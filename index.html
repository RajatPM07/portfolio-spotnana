Case Study 01

Ria: AI underwriting for health insurance

Turning a 3 hour manual review into a governed AI decision, without ever putting trust on credit.

1.1 The problem, why, who, and validation

The problem
About 45 percent of ICICI Lombard's monthly health insurance proposals were non standard cases that could not pass straight through, every one needed manual underwriting review.
Why it hurt
Each review took about 3 hours of an underwriter's time, which meant nearly half of all incoming volume was routed through the slowest path in the funnel.
Why now
Fixing it had become a CXO level strategic priority, with the work cutting across 8 teams and 5 integrated platforms.
Who for
The underwriters carrying the manual load, and every customer whose proposal sat waiting on a 3 hour review.
Validation

1.3 How we earned the right to automate

This is the part of the story I am most careful about, because it is easy to overclaim. In 2026, saying you shipped an LLM feature is table stakes. The differentiating work is the governance wrapped around it. Ria is not full automation with a safety net hidden somewhere. It is a phased rollout behind human in the loop review gates, where review coverage only decreases after specific trust metrics clear their bar.

Phase 1, complete

100 percent human review

Every AI verdict sat behind a human underwriter. This phase existed to generate the evidence, not to save time.

Gate cleared, review coverage allowed to decrease LLM to underwriter agreement: 95 percent, against a 92 percent bar Dangerous miss rate, AI more lenient than a human: targeted at near zero

The dangerous miss rate is the number I watch hardest, because it measures harm, not just accuracy.

Phase 2, live now

Risk tiered review

Lower risk tiers carry lighter review while high risk cases keep a full human pass. Coverage came down only because the gate above cleared. Trust was earned, not assumed.

Next gate, in active buildout, not yet live Fairness disparity thresholds across groups Verdict to transcript traceability, every decision explainable back to its evidence
Phase 3, planned

Wider automation, only if earned

The next reduction in review coverage waits on the gate above. Both requirements are in buildout right now, and I would rather say that plainly than claim them early.

The broader frame: this rollout sits inside a governance framework spanning 7 domains: decision integrity, data protection and privacy, consent integrity, human oversight and accountability, security and abuse resistance, duty of care and interaction safety, and third party and vendor risk. Some of it is live, some of it is a target we are building toward, and the roadmap is honest about which is which. Naming an AI pattern is easy. Operating a framework like this in a regulated industry, where speed can never come at the cost of trust or data integrity, is the discipline I would bring to Spotnana.

Case Study 03

Elevate and the recommendation engine

A market share strategy on a 1,500 crore rupee portfolio, and the ML engine that turned it into a decision for every customer.

3.1 The problem, why, who, and validation

In FY24-25 I owned Elevate, the growth strategy for a retail health insurance portfolio of about 1,500 crore rupees holding 2.3 percent market share. Two levers had real headroom. The first was competitive positioning, where an assessment of the landscape surfaced gaps the portfolio could credibly close. The second was the existing customer base, where cross sell offers were accepted just 1.5 percent of the time and there was no systematic way to decide which customer should get which action next.

The problem
A 1,500 crore portfolio at 2.3 percent share, with growth gated on positioning gaps and an untapped existing customer base.
Why it hurt
A 1.5 percent cross sell acceptance baseline meant nearly every offer shown to an existing customer was the wrong offer, wasted attention on both sides.
Why now
The competitive assessment made the positioning gaps concrete, and FY24-25 was the cycle to close them before competitors did.
Who for
Existing policyholders who deserved a relevant next offer instead of a generic one, and the customer segments addressed by new coverage architecture and pricing.
Validation
I authored the detailed product requirements documents defining coverage architecture and pricing across customer segments, and mentored one Associate PM to independent product ownership along the way.

The engine behind these numbers is a production ML recommendation system I shipped in partnership with an applied science team, working together on feature engineering, evaluation metrics, and production guardrails. One model run answers both questions that matter for an existing customer: are they at risk of leaving, and what should we offer them next.

Scores do not sit in a dashboard waiting for someone to act. They feed the 1 click cross sell surface inside the checkout flow, which carried a hard design constraint from day one: zero cannibalization of the core purchase journey. It cleared that bar while delivering an 18 percent click through rate and a 10 percent lift in average revenue per user.

Why this maps to Spotnana: this role names recommendations and personalization as one of its pillars, driving the engine that decides what travelers see first. That is, underneath, a next best action problem. Recommending the right flight, hotel, or ancillary at the right moment is the same shape of work as this engine: propensity modeling, cohort targeting, and attach economics inside a checkout flow that must never degrade the core journey. I have shipped that discipline to production once, and I would like to do it where the actions are trips.
